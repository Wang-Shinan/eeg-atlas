A published accuracy figure for an EEG foundation model is a claim about a
specific model, on a specific dataset, under a specific adaptation protocol,
with a specific channel-handling pipeline. Change any one of those and the
number moves. This chapter sets out the dimensions that determine whether two
such numbers can be compared at all.

## pretraining overlap

The first question to ask about any reported transfer result is whether the
downstream dataset was part of the pretraining corpus.

Public EEG corpora are heavily reused. The large clinical archives that make
pretraining possible are also the source of several standard evaluation tasks,
so a model pretrained on "all available public EEG" has plausibly already seen
the recordings it is later tested on. When that happens, a strong downstream
score is partly a memorisation result, and the comparison against a model
pretrained on a disjoint corpus is not meaningful.

Individual papers often handle this for their own model by excluding downstream
datasets from pretraining. What is much rarer is an *audit across models*: when a
benchmark evaluates ten independently pretrained checkpoints, each with its own
undocumented corpus, overlap has to be established per model–dataset pair rather
than assumed away.

## channel handling

As the previous chapter described, models divide into fixed- and
flexible-channel designs. A benchmark has to decide how to feed heterogeneous
datasets to both, and every available option embeds a bias:

- **Map everything to a common montage.** Comparable inputs, but discards the
  flexible models' advantage and throws away channels on high-density data.
- **Give each model its native format.** Plays to each design's strength, but
  the models no longer see the same information.
- **Exclude models that cannot handle the format.** Clean comparison among
  those that remain, and silently narrows the field — convolutional tokenizers
  with a hard-coded channel count are the usual casualty.

None of these is wrong. What matters is that the choice is stated, because it
determines which architectural family the benchmark structurally favours.

Flat channels are not a neutral absence — they are a signal. On real EEG, 17
simultaneously isoelectric channels would be a profound abnormality, and a model
pretrained on intact recordings has learned to treat near-zero amplitude as
meaningful. The zero-padded input therefore falls far outside the pretraining
distribution, and the resulting score reflects the model's response to an
artificial condition rather than its ability to use the two real channels. This
is why explicit masking, where the model is told which positions carry no
electrode, behaves differently from zero-filling.

## robustness to channel corruption

Clinical recordings lose channels. Electrodes come loose, impedances drift, and
long-term monitoring routinely runs with a bad lead for hours. A model intended
for clinical use should degrade gracefully as channels drop out.

Several papers report some version of this, but usually as a property of their
own model on one or two tasks. Evaluating it benchmark-wide — applying the same
channel masks across every task and model — turns an architectural anecdote into
a comparable measurement, and it is the dimension where flexible-channel designs
have the clearest theoretical advantage.

## adaptation with few or no labels

Since the motivation for pretraining is label scarcity, the adaptation protocol
is not a detail; it is the point.

**Full fine-tuning** on the complete labelled training set measures the ceiling,
but it is the setting where pretraining matters least — with enough labels, a
well-tuned supervised baseline is often competitive. **Few-shot** adaptation,
using a handful of labelled examples per class, tests the actual claim.
**Zero-shot** transfer, with no downstream labels at all, tests whether the
representation is clinically structured on its own.

Two distinctions are easy to blur. Zero-shot *reconstruction* on an unseen
device is a signal-modelling result, not a task-adaptation result. And
leave-one-subject-out evaluation measures cross-subject generalisation, which is
a different axis from label efficiency — a model can be excellent at one and
poor at the other.

## what the tasks are

Finally, a benchmark's task list encodes a view of what EEG models are for.
Collections assembled from BCI paradigms — motor imagery, SSVEP, P300 — measure
something quite different from collections built around clinical reads, such as
abnormality detection, seizure and IED detection, sleep staging, and
encephalopathy grading. A model can lead on one family and trail on the other,
so an aggregate "average accuracy" across a mixed collection is largely a
statement about the collection's composition.

Organising tasks into an explicit taxonomy — families of clinical capability,
subdivided into specific task types — makes this visible, and lets a result be
read as "strong on paroxysmal event detection, weak on continuous state
estimation" rather than as a single ranking.

Their task compositions differ. If one benchmark is weighted toward BCI

short, stimulus-locked, single-subject decoding will rank highly on the first
and poorly on the second — and averaging over each collection produces two
honest but incomparable rankings. This is the practical reason to report results
per task family instead of, or in addition to, an aggregate: the aggregate is a
property of the benchmark's composition as much as of the models.

## reading a benchmark table

Put together, the checklist for any reported result is:

1. Was the downstream dataset audited against this model's pretraining corpus?
2. How were heterogeneous montages fed to models with different channel assumptions?
3. Was robustness tested under a channel-corruption protocol applied to all models?
4. Which adaptation regime produced the number — full, few-shot, or zero-shot?
5. What is the task composition, and is the result broken down by capability?

Few published evaluations satisfy all five, and that is not a criticism of any
one paper — each was built to answer a narrower question. It is a reason to read
the protocol section before the results table.
